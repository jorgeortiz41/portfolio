Growth is still growth, even at 0.4%. That's the headline coming out of Puerto Rico's Planning Board this month, which has maintained its forecast of a modest economic slowdown for the island through 2026 and 2027. For anyone building a business, running a startup, or evaluating Puerto Rico as a place to invest or relocate a tech operation, the details behind that number matter more than the number itself.

## The Numbers, in Context

Under its baseline scenario, the Planning Board projects Puerto Rico's real Gross Product growing just 0.4% in 2026 and easing further to 0.3% in 2027. That's a deceleration, not a contraction — but the board also modeled a wider range of outcomes. In an optimistic scenario, growth could reach 2.3% in 2026 and 1% in 2027; in a pessimistic one, the island could see the economy contract by 1.6% in 2026 and 0.5% in 2027. That spread itself tells a story: this is an economy where the range of plausible outcomes is unusually wide, driven largely by factors outside local control.

Planning Board President Héctor Morales-Martínez framed the situation in terms of resilience rather than alarm, noting that "Puerto Rico's economy continues to show signs of resilience, even in a volatile global environment and in the face of reduced federal funding." That's a notably measured tone for a forecast that's technically flat-to-declining, and it reflects a broader pattern in how the island's economic institutions have been talking about growth over the past year: slower, yes, but not sinking.

## What's Actually Driving the Slowdown

The single biggest headwind cited across multiple analyses is the decline in federal disaster recovery funding. In the years following Hurricane Maria and subsequent disasters, federal reconstruction dollars — from CDBG-DR allocations and related programs — acted as a significant artificial stimulus to the island's construction and services sectors. As that funding tapers, the economy loses a source of activity that isn't easily replaced by organic private investment overnight.

The Planning Board's own language captures the tension directly: "even with fewer federal funds and a tighter fiscal environment, tourism and construction have kept the economy moving." In other words, the slowdown isn't a story of broad economic weakness — it's a story of one major tailwind (federal reconstruction spending) fading while other sectors work to pick up the slack.

## The Sectors Carrying the Load

Three sectors stand out in the Planning Board's analysis as the forces keeping growth in positive territory despite that federal pullback.

Tourism remains a genuine stabilizer, with continued visitor arrivals and ongoing lodging expansion providing a steady, less federally-dependent source of economic activity. Construction, somewhat counterintuitively, is still contributing positively — not purely from disaster recovery anymore, but increasingly from infrastructure projects that have taken on a life of their own beyond the initial reconstruction mandate.

The most interesting piece for anyone watching the island's business and technology sector is manufacturing. The forecast specifically credits Executive Order 2025-012 and associated permitting reforms with attracting new manufacturing interest to the island. Permitting speed has long been cited by businesses — in manufacturing, pharma, and tech alike — as one of the more frustrating friction points in setting up operations in Puerto Rico, so regulatory streamlining that's specifically credited with drawing new investment is a meaningful signal, not just a bureaucratic footnote. Combined with a broader push toward economic diversification and reshoring, it suggests Puerto Rico's economic planners are trying to build sources of growth that don't depend on the next hurricane's federal aid package.

## Why This Matters Beyond the Spreadsheet

For founders, technologists, and business owners weighing Puerto Rico — whether for Act 60 tax incentives, its growing reputation as a nearshore tech and services hub, or simply as a place to build — this forecast is a useful reality check against both the overly bullish and overly bearish narratives that tend to circulate about the island's economy. It's neither the runaway growth story that some incentive-driven investment pitches imply, nor the crisis narrative that headlines about federal funding cuts might suggest on their own.

What it does suggest is an economy in transition: moving, deliberately if slowly, from a post-disaster, federally-subsidized recovery model toward one anchored more in tourism resilience, targeted regulatory reform, and diversified private investment. For a tech sector that's been quietly growing on the island — bootcamps, startups, and remote-friendly companies increasingly citing Puerto Rico as a viable base — a stable, if modest, growth environment paired with actual permitting reform is arguably a healthier foundation to build on than another temporary federal-dollar sugar high.

## Takeaway

Puerto Rico isn't booming, but according to its own Planning Board, it isn't stalling either — it's shifting from disaster-recovery-fueled growth toward a leaner mix of tourism, construction, and newly-incentivized manufacturing investment. The permitting reforms under Executive Order 2025-012 are worth watching closely: if they continue attracting manufacturing and diversified investment as federal reconstruction dollars recede, that could prove to be the more durable growth story for the island's business and tech community over the next few years.
