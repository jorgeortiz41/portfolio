Here's a puzzle that should catch the attention of anyone watching Puerto Rico's economy: how does a telecom company post its third straight quarter of subscriber growth, reduce customer churn, and swing from a massive operating loss to a profit — and then get its credit rating cut deeper into distressed territory a few weeks later? That's exactly what happened to Liberty Communications of Puerto Rico this month, and the gap between its operational story and its financial one says a lot about the pressures facing the island's biggest infrastructure providers right now.

## The Downgrade

On August 21, 2026, Fitch Ratings downgraded Liberty Puerto Rico's long-term issuer default rating from "CCC" to "CC" — a rating deep in distressed-debt territory that signals the agency sees default as a real possibility rather than a remote tail risk. Fitch also cut its rating on the company's term loans and secured notes maturing between 2027 and 2029, from "CCC+" down to "CCC-".

The reasoning comes down to a debt structure that's becoming harder to sustain. Liberty carries roughly $3.1 billion in total debt, and $1.2 billion of that — 38% of the total — comes due in October 2027 in the form of senior secured notes. Layer on another $620 million maturing in 2028 and $820 million in 2029, and you get a company facing a wall of obligations that Fitch says its cash flow can't currently clear. The agency specifically flagged negative free cash flow projected through 2026 and 2027, driven largely by high interest expense, and concluded that available cash and committed credit facilities likely won't be enough to cover the 2027 maturity without some kind of restructuring — Fitch's language pointed toward a debt exchange as the likely path forward.

## The Part That Doesn't Fit the Narrative

What makes this story more than a routine credit-rating headline is the contrast with Liberty's actual operating performance. Just weeks earlier, the company reported second-quarter 2026 results that told a genuinely encouraging story: operating income of $23.3 million, a dramatic swing from an operating loss of $487.6 million a year earlier (a comparison skewed by a one-time $494 million spectrum asset impairment, but a real improvement nonetheless). Adjusted operating income before depreciation rose 7% to $92.7 million even as revenue slipped 5% to $287.5 million, pointing to genuine gains in operational efficiency.

More tellingly, Liberty logged its third consecutive quarter of postpaid mobile subscriber growth and its second straight quarter of net video subscriber additions, while broadband churn declined for a third straight quarter. The company also put real money behind its network: $45 million invested in property and equipment during the quarter, expanding broadband access to 5,900 homes compared with just 900 homes the year before. General Manager Ignacio Román pointed to this trend explicitly, noting the company's continued momentum in reducing churn and growing its subscriber base.

Fitch doesn't dispute any of this. The agency's own analysis expects Liberty's net leverage to improve from roughly 8.6x EBITDA down to 8x by 2027 as these operational gains continue. The problem, in Fitch's assessment, is simply one of timing and magnitude: even with real operational progress, leverage isn't projected to come down fast enough or far enough to comfortably clear that October 2027 maturity wall. Good execution on the ground doesn't automatically solve a balance sheet built during a much different period for the business.

## Why This Matters for Puerto Rico

Liberty is one of the largest telecommunications providers on the island, meaning its financial health isn't just a Wall Street story — it's an infrastructure story. Broadband expansion, mobile network quality, and video service reliability for a large share of Puerto Rico's residents and businesses run through this company's balance sheet. A downgrade signaling default risk raises real questions about the company's ability to keep funding network investment at its current pace if it needs to prioritize cash toward debt restructuring instead.

It's also a window into a broader dynamic on the island: companies delivering genuinely improving fundamentals — more customers, better retention, expanding infrastructure — that are nonetheless still working through debt loads shaped by an earlier, more difficult chapter. Puerto Rico's overall economic backdrop has been one of persistently modest growth for years, and capital-intensive infrastructure businesses like telecom carriers are often where that tension between "operating better" and "still financially stretched" shows up most visibly.

Liberty's own response leans on exactly this distinction. Román has framed the company's priority as building "a sustainable capital structure" while pointing to its "tremendous assets" and "strong commercial brands" as reasons for confidence — language that reads as an acknowledgment that a financial restructuring, likely some form of debt exchange, is now the expected next chapter rather than a worst-case scenario.

## The Takeaway

Liberty Puerto Rico's situation is a reminder that operational improvement and financial stability don't always move on the same timeline. The company is doing real things right — growing its subscriber base, cutting churn, investing in network expansion — and getting punished on its credit rating anyway, because a 2027 debt wall doesn't care how good this quarter's numbers looked. For Puerto Rico watchers, the story worth following from here isn't whether Liberty can keep growing subscribers; it's whether it can restructure that debt load on terms that let it keep investing in the island's network at all. That outcome will matter to a lot more people than bondholders.
