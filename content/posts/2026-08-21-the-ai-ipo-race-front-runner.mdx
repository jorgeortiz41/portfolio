For the better part of two years, the question hanging over the AI industry hasn't been "if" the frontier labs would go public — it's been "who first, and when." As of this week, that question has an answer that might surprise people who assumed OpenAI, with its cultural dominance and consumer reach, would naturally win the race to Wall Street. Instead, it's looking like Anthropic could beat its larger rival to the public markets by months, with paperwork potentially filed before August is even over.

It's a story that says as much about the state of the AI business as it does about either individual company — and it marks what may be remembered as the moment the "private frontier lab" era quietly ended.

## The State of Play

According to reporting this week, Anthropic may publicly file its IPO paperwork before the end of August 2026. That would follow a confidential draft S-1 the company submitted to the SEC back in June — a standard preliminary step for companies preparing to go public without yet tipping their hand to competitors. The numbers behind that filing are striking: Anthropic's revenue run rate has reportedly surpassed $65 billion, with the company posting $11.5 billion in quarterly revenue — a roughly fourteen-fold increase year over year.

For context on just how fast that is, consider the benchmark being used to size up the eventual listing: SpaceX's $75 billion public float in June 2026, described at the time as the largest technology flotation on record. Anthropic's IPO is now being talked about in the same breath.

OpenAI, meanwhile, is playing a different game on a different clock. The company's CFO has pointed to 2027 as the likely year it goes public, and some reporting suggests it may not happen until "the closing weeks" of this year at the earliest. OpenAI's most recent quarter showed $6.2 billion in revenue with 18% sequential growth — solid, but notably slower than Anthropic's trajectory, and against a revenue run rate that's separately been reported crossing $40 billion. OpenAI is still, by most measures, the more culturally dominant AI product — but on the specific metric of "who's ready for public markets first," Anthropic currently looks like the one further along.

## Why Would Either Company Want This?

Going public is not a small decision for an AI lab. It means quarterly earnings calls, activist investors, public disclosure of costs and margins that have mostly stayed opaque, and a governance structure now open to scrutiny — Anthropic's is notably built around supervoting shares that keep founder control intact even as public shareholders buy in. So why do it at all?

The obvious answer is capital. Training frontier models, building and leasing data center capacity, and competing for scarce AI chips is extraordinarily expensive, and private fundraising rounds — however large — eventually hit practical ceilings that public markets don't have. There's also a talent and credibility dimension: a public listing gives employees liquidity for equity compensation and signals a kind of institutional maturity that can matter when courting enterprise customers and government contracts.

That last point is complicated in Anthropic's case by reported tensions with the Trump administration over military applications of its models — a reminder that going public doesn't just invite investor scrutiny, it invites political scrutiny too, at a moment when AI policy is anything but settled.

## What It Means for the Rest of the Industry

This isn't just a two-company story. The AI sector this week also saw Nvidia strike a $6 billion licensing deal plus a $1 billion investment in the startup Poolside, Stripe acquire the model-routing gateway OpenRouter, and Anthropic's own enterprise arm acquire the consultancy Fractional AI — all signs of an industry consolidating and maturing at a pace that outstrips its very recent past as a research curiosity. When the two best-funded, most-watched AI labs on the planet are both racing toward public markets within the same year, it reshapes the competitive picture for everyone downstream: enterprise buyers evaluating AI vendors, developers choosing which API to build on, and investors trying to figure out where the actual moats are in a field that's been defined more by capital intensity than defensibility.

It also puts real financial transparency into a market that has, until now, run almost entirely on reported valuations and leaked fundraising terms. Once S-1 filings become public, the rest of the industry — and the public — gets an unusually clear look at what it actually costs to run a frontier AI lab, and what kind of revenue growth is required to justify it.

## The Takeaway

Whether Anthropic files by month's end or slips into September, the direction of travel is now unmistakable: the two companies that have defined this era of AI are both headed for public markets, and one of them looks likely to get there well before most people expected. For an industry that has operated largely behind the curtain of private valuations, that's a genuinely significant shift — and it's worth watching closely, because the numbers these companies are about to disclose will shape how everyone thinks about AI economics for years to come.
